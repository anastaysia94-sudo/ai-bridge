## Tonal revision after the user named references

The show is a gothic comic mystery with active stage business: fluttering paper, aggressive projector clicks, deadlines, absurdly cheerful conclusion slides, sudden neon against velvet darkness, and then one earned quiet beat. Mara is curious and wry; Ivo has dry deadline humor; June is exact without being cold. The world and all characters remain original. Avoid recognizable iconography or sequences from the cited works.

## One sentence

In a city where unfinished questions become doors, a community projectionist, a skeptical director, and an archivist must close one door each episode while the projector reveals a harder question hiding behind it.

## Why it can continue

Each episode has a small answer and a lasting consequence. A question appears in an ordinary place. The team must verify what happened, make a choice and pay a cost. They close the local door, then discover what the answer changes in their relationships or in the city. Episodes can stand alone for a new viewer because the local problem has a clear beginning, middle and end. A season arc accumulates choices; no episode depends on an endless string of unanswered cliffhangers. The series can stop with a satisfying ending at any season gate. “Neverending” describes a repeatable story engine, not a promise of infinite automated publishing.

## Rules of the world

1. Questions appear as projected subtitles on objects only when two people honestly disagree about what the object means. The projection never declares a fact.
2. A door opens when the team tests a claim in the real world. It stays open until they record the evidence, the uncertainty and a decision.
3. The projector can reveal memories of actions but cannot read thoughts or predict the future. Its gaps matter.
4. A closed door changes one concrete thing in the city: a route, an agreement, a piece of public art or a relationship. No reset at the end of the episode.
5. The team cannot solve a conflict by humiliating the person who asked the question. The series values curiosity, humor and consent.
6. A trend word is never a magical object by itself. It becomes useful only if it fits a character's present need, the established rules and the episode's emotional question.
7. Every episode ends with a ledger entry: what they thought, what they learned, what changed and what remains open. Previous ledger entries are immutable history; later discoveries may reinterpret them.

## Cast and production roles

**Mara Vale, projectionist and viewpoint character.** Quick with light, slower with certainty. Wants to find her missing mentor. Strength: can show people exactly what she observed. Flaw: rushes to finish a story for others. Her arc is learning to leave room for another person's answer. First-person scenes can use her voice, but other characters keep their own motives.

**Ivo Reed, director of the municipal night theatre.** Keeps productions on time, guards the building's fragile funding and challenges grand claims. He wants the theatre to survive; his scheduling choices can clash with Mara's investigations. He is neither a villain nor a mouthpiece.

**June Arroyo, archivist and dramaturg.** Tracks citations, contradictions and physical records. Wants public access to the archive without exposing private lives. She can say “unknown” without surrendering the scene.

**The Presenter.** An offstage or shadow character who projects dense slides and asks the same question in increasingly specific ways. The Performer in the chair listens, takes notes and eventually chooses to interrupt with a better question. No physical captivity needs to be enacted; the pressure comes from the endless presentation and a ticking show call.

**Producer role, in-universe and in production:** selects a bounded episode promise, rights-cleared assets, budget and release gate. **Director role:** controls action, tempo, blocking and reveal. **Dramaturg/research role:** verifies facts and continuity. **Performer role:** makes each character's objective visible. **Editor role:** builds separate vertical and landscape cuts. **Sound and access role:** captures clean dialogue, captions, transcript and readable visuals. **Publisher role:** prepares the blog and platform metadata, then verifies actual publication. These roles can be one person wearing separate review hats; their checklists should remain distinct.

## Season one spine — seven question doors

1. **The Door in the Notes:** Who owns the ending to a story everyone witnessed? Mara learns that the projected record omits a crucial pause.
2. **The Price of Perfect:** A slide promises Mara a flawless forever if the theatre earns $10,000 by midnight. The team sells 100 real fictional tickets without surrendering archive rights; money solves a concrete need, while the promise of perfection fails its evidence test.
3. **The Map That Moved:** A marked route changes overnight. Ivo wants to reopen the theatre by morning; June needs a source for the map. The local route is resolved; a citywide signal emerges.
4. **The Borrowed Voice:** A recording sounds like the mentor. Mara must prove origin before sharing it. The team's trust bends.
5. **The Audience of One:** A public performance contains a private message. June protects the person while finding a lawful way to show the truth.
6. **The Quiet Rehearsal:** Ivo's budget ledger and Mara's story ledger disagree. They both have incomplete evidence. A choice costs them a venue.
7. **The Final Projector:** The mentor's disappearance gets a grounded answer. The team can preserve or retire the machine; the season ends either way. A new season, if made, follows the changed people and a new question, not an artificially delayed answer.

## Story quality gate

Can a newcomer understand the local question in the first ten seconds? Does someone make a consequential choice rather than simply explain a theme? Does the answer change a future episode? Is the ending satisfying even if no one watches the next video? Is the trend word removable without breaking the plot? If removing it improves the story, remove it. Can a performer play the scene with normal props and an affordable location? Are any facts, rights or real-world references unverified? Fix those before publication.
