# 100 checks before building or releasing this story series

Checkpoint: October 4, 2026 Pacific. These are decisions and checks, not a claim that all 100 have been done. Items 1–20, 31–40, 51–60, 71–80 and 91–100 contain essential gates for the first public episode; the rest guide scale and later seasons. Do not let an unneeded scaling question delay a small, safe, complete pilot. Record answers and evidence beside each item as work resumes.

## Purpose and audience (1–10)

1. State the series promise in one sentence a new viewer understands.
2. Choose the primary audience by age, interests and viewing context.
3. Decide whether the first release is theatre, animation, live action or a hybrid.
4. Name the emotional payoff of episode one.
5. Define what viewers should remember after one Short.
6. Choose the lead character's objective independent of any trend.
7. Name the viewer need the blog answers beyond “watch the video.”
8. Decide whether the series is family friendly or intended for older viewers.
9. List hard content boundaries for violence, distress, profanity and flashing images.
10. Write a one-page statement of what would make the pilot worth continuing.

## References and originality (11–20)

11. Confirm which “Alice Revisited” game the user means.
12. Capture the user's exact favorite elements of the Alice reference.
13. Capture the user's exact favorite elements of *Lollipop Chainsaw*.
14. List every other reference work with title, medium and creator/publisher.
15. Extract high-level emotional or tonal lessons from each reference.
16. Check new characters for resemblance to protected characters.
17. Check the plot sequence for near-copying distinctive scenes.
18. Check costumes, props, color arrangements and logos for recognizable copies.
19. Check dialogue and catchphrases for direct or close borrowing.
20. Keep an inspiration register explaining each original transformation.

## Research and trends (21–30)

21. Choose Google Trends geography before making a “top ten” claim.
22. Choose a comparable time window before ranking queries.
23. Choose YouTube Search or Web Search; report them separately.
24. Distinguish search terms, topics, rising queries and trending searches.
25. Save the exact query spellings and comparison groups.
26. Use a common anchor if comparing multiple Trends groups.
27. Record the checked time and original Trends URL/export.
28. Treat normalized index values as relative interest, not search volume.
29. Mark suppressed, missing or incomparable data instead of filling gaps.
30. Verify whether a trend serves the actual story audience before using it.

## Story architecture and continuity (31–40)

31. Lock the rules for the projector and question doors.
32. Define what the projector cannot know or do.
33. Give each major character a goal, flaw and ability to change.
34. Define the local question that episode one resolves.
35. Define the season question that episode one advances.
36. Give each scene a conflict, action and changed state.
37. Check that episode one has a satisfying ending without episode two.
38. Record each lasting change in the canon ledger.
39. Keep unknown facts explicitly unknown until the story earns an answer.
40. Establish a review gate before promoting a draft to canon.

## Script and performance (41–50)

41. Read the play aloud and measure actual duration.
42. Cut speeches that repeat an on-screen fact without adding conflict.
43. Give Mara a playable objective in every scene.
44. Give Ivo a concrete time or resource constraint.
45. Give June a choice beyond reciting evidence.
46. Give the Presenter a clear dramatic function rather than generic menace.
47. Make the chair frame a voluntary performance choice on stage.
48. Decide where dark comedy releases tension and where silence returns it.
49. Confirm that the viewer can follow the plot without reading fine print.
50. Test whether the first ten seconds show a real visual question.

## Rights, safety and people (51–60)

51. Secure permission for the filming/performance location.
52. Obtain performer releases appropriate to the intended channels.
53. Confirm all displayed photographs and graphics are original or licensed.
54. Confirm original or cross-platform rights for music and sound effects.
55. Keep real private records and identifying details off set.
56. Avoid physical restraint in the chair scene; stage pressure through performance.
57. Review flashing-light, projection and sound-intensity comfort.
58. Decide content warnings based on the actual final cut.
59. Confirm safe prop use and clear floor paths during paper effects.
60. Keep a rights and consent log with the master assets.

## Budget and feasibility (61–70)

61. Inventory equipment already owned or freely available.
62. Estimate rehearsal, filming, editing and caption labor hours.
63. Price location, transport, storage and distribution costs before commitments.
64. Set a zero-new-spend pilot path if no cash is available.
65. Identify which jobs one person can combine without losing quality.
66. Choose effects reproducible with practical light and editing.
67. Save an edit plan for portrait and a separate one for landscape.
68. Define the smallest complete pilot if time or people disappear.
69. Set a stop rule if costs or rehearsal time exceed the cap.
70. Check whether proposed monetization can exist before counting revenue.

## Video and access (71–80)

71. Confirm current upload duration and format in each actual account.
72. Export a real 1080×1920 portrait master when the source supports it.
73. Export a separately composed 1920×1080 landscape master.
74. Build a smaller 720p web preview if self-hosting is appropriate.
75. Keep H.264/AAC MP4 and faststart for broad compatibility.
76. Proof captions against the finished dialogue, not the draft script.
77. Provide a transcript and readable blog version.
78. Check mobile caption placement against platform controls.
79. Watch the cut on a phone and desktop, with and without sound.
80. Inspect final audio peaks, black frames, text legibility and file size.

## Distribution and discovery (81–90)

81. Identify the exact YouTube, Facebook and blog accounts to use.
82. Confirm whether WordPress, Blogger or “Hermes [2026]” is the actual blog platform.
83. Write original titles that describe the story honestly.
84. Make a thumbnail whose promise the episode fulfills.
85. Add live links only after publishing and checking them.
86. Decide how the Short sends interested viewers to the full play.
87. Plan a text-first blog page that loads without a heavy autoplay embed.
88. Draft platform-specific captions and audience prompts.
89. Record publish time, URL, account and version for each release.
90. Keep a backup and replacement path if an asset must be corrected.

## Learning and scale (91–100)

91. Define one primary audience metric for the pilot before release.
92. Record actual impressions, views and retention at fixed intervals.
93. Separate viewing behavior from clicks, purchases and profit.
94. Ask whether viewers understood the local resolution.
95. Track exact comments or feedback without pretending one comment represents everyone.
96. Decide keep, revise or stop based on evidence and production cost.
97. Update canon only after the released episode passes review.
98. Preserve source files, cut lists, captions, rights and evidence with the episode.
99. Recheck references and trend evidence before each new season.
100. Choose the next episode from character consequence and audience learning, then repeat the gate.
